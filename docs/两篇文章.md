Socioeconomic Disadvantage and Incident Frailty Across Health Systems

Abstract
Importance: Socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with frailty, but the pathways linking disadvantage to functional decline may differ across health care systems.
Objective: To examine whether health care access and depression mediate the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and incident frailty across 3 national health care settings, and to assess the temporal relationship between frailty and depressive symptoms.
Design, Setting, and Participants: This longitudinal cohort study used harmonized data from 3 nationally representative studies of adults aged 50 years or older: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, and the US Health and Retirement Study. Analyses included Cox proportional hazards models, serial mediation analysis, and panel graphical vector autoregression network analysis across 4 survey waves.
Exposure: Socioeconomic status, assessed using harmonized indicators of education, income or wealth, and related socioeconomic resources.
Main Outcomes and Measures: Incident frailty, defined using a deficit-accumulation frailty index score of 0.25 or greater. Mediators included health care access and depressive symptoms. Temporal network models examined directed associations between frailty deficits and depressive symptoms over time.
Results: The study included 11 263 participants from China, 9560 from England, and 19 310 from the United States. Higher SES was associated with a lower risk of incident frailty in all 3 cohorts after full adjustment, with hazard ratios per 1-SD increase in SES of 0.56 in China (95% CI, 0.51-0.61), 0.54 in England (95% CI, 0.50-0.59), and 0.66 in the United States (95% CI, 0.61-0.70). The mediated association through health care access and depression differed across settings. The standardized indirect association was smallest in China (β = −0.066), largest in England (β = −0.197), and intermediate in the United States (β = −0.103). Mediation patterns suggested balanced contributions from health care access and depression in England, depression-dominant mediation in the United States, and a larger residual direct association in China. In temporal network analyses, frailty was the dominant predictor of subsequent depressive symptoms across cohorts, with weaker reciprocal associations from depression to later frailty.
Conclusions and Relevance: In this longitudinal analysis of 3 nationally representative ageing cohorts, socioeconomic disadvantage was consistently associated with higher frailty risk, but the mediating pathways differed by national context. These findings suggest that frailty prevention strategies may need to be tailored to health system and social policy contexts, including reducing barriers to health care engagement, integrating depression screening into geriatric care, and addressing broader structural determinants of healthy ageing.
